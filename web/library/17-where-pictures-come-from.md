Every picture in a game is called an *asset*. So are the sounds, the
music, and the words. Making them is *art* and *audio*, and the people
who make pictures are *artists*.

* * *

The studio's pictures came from two places. Some were drawn to order
for VEFR. Others were painted by picture helpers that followed a written
style guide and a few finished pictures to copy the feel of.

Studios call the style guide a *style bible*. The pictures you show a
painter to match are *reference images*. Keeping every picture looking
like it came from the same hand is *art direction*.

* * *

Every helper-painted picture has its exact instructions saved right
beside the original. Where a thing came from is its *provenance*.

It matters. If someone asks "who made this, and are we allowed to use
it?", you can always answer. The rule for who may use it is its
*licence*. VEFR's own studio art uses one called *CC BY-SA*: share it,
change it, but say where it came from.

* * *

The big originals are too heavy for a web page. So each one is shrunk
and saved as a small file: portraits are 320 dots wide, map tiles 96.
Shrinking a file is *compression*; the dots are *pixels*; the kind of
file (here, *WebP*) is its *format*.

* * *

A resident's picture has no box around it. The background was cut
away, dot by dot, and those dots were made fully see-through. That's a
*cutout*, and see-through is stored as *alpha* (the book about layers
tells you more).

A picture made of many small pictures side by side, like a flip book,
is a *sprite sheet*. That's how game characters walk.

* * *

**Try it.** Open the Map Room and look closely at a picture tile. Every
tile is one square picture, drawn to fit its neighbours with no seams.
Artists call that *tileable*.

the studio is teaching its own tiny painter.
