Every story is someone who wants something, and something in the way.
Writers call the first part the *premise*. That's enough to start.

Writing for games is called *narrative design*. It happens at the Desk,
in the Workshop.

* * *

Most stories walk the same road, in the same big steps:

1. Home, where things are ordinary.
2. A call to leave.
3. The first step over the line.
4. Trouble, friends, and tests.
5. The worst moment.
6. Coming home changed.

This road is called *the hero's journey*. The big steps are *beats*.
You don't have to follow it, but it's a good map when you're lost.

* * *

Before you write any scenes, write down the rules of your world. What's
true? What never happens? That file is your world's *canon*. In VEFR
it's the logbok, and the engine reads it before it writes a word.

Writers call the whole collection of facts a *story bible*. Game
studios call it *lore*.

* * *

In a game, the player chooses. A choice that changes what happens next
is a *branch*. A story full of branches is *branching narrative*.

When the game remembers a choice ("you already met the keeper"), that
memory is a *flag*: one yes-or-no the story can check later.

Characters' lines live in the voices folder. Talking back and forth is
*dialogue*; when the player's answers split the talk, it's a
*dialogue tree*.

* * *

Stuck? Everyone gets stuck. It's called *writer's block*, and the
trick is to write something small and wrong on purpose, then fix it.
That's a *first draft*. Fixing it is *revising*.

You can ask the Storyteller what happens next. It's only a
suggestion: you choose what stays, and your story stays yours.

* * *

**Try it.** Open the Workshop. Write one sentence: who your hero is,
and what they want. Then one more: what's in the way.

That's a premise. Every book in this Library started exactly there.
