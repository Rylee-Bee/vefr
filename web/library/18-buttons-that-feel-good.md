Everything you press, read, or hear in a game is its *user interface*,
or *UI*. How it *feels* to use is the *user experience*, or *UX*.
People who design them are *UI* and *UX designers*.

* * *

A good button tells you it's a button before you press it: a shape, a
border, a word that says what happens. Designers call that an
*affordance*, a thing that shows how to use it.

Button words should be actions: "Check the map", "Keep this note".
Never just "OK".

* * *

When you press something, the game should answer right away. A squeak,
a colour, a line of words. That answer is *feedback*.

Press **Check the map** and watch: the words change to "Checking the
map…" at once, before the answer comes back. Nothing feels broken while
you wait.

* * *

Buttons have to be big enough for a thumb. Studios measure: 44 dots
square is the smallest comfortable target. That rule is part of
*accessibility*, making sure everyone can play.

Accessibility also means: words dark enough on their background to
read easily (*contrast*), every picture described in words for people
who can't see it (*alt text*), and nothing that flashes or zooms if you
don't want it.

* * *

The Boiler Room (Settings) lets you turn motion down and sound off.
Those are *preferences*. A game that listens to them is being polite.

The "Keep it simple / Show me how things work" slider is one too. Hiding
the complicated parts until someone asks is called *progressive
disclosure*.

* * *

**Try it.** Go to the Boiler Room and set motion to Off. Then walk
through the rooms. Everything still works; it just holds still.

Now try the studio with only the keyboard: Tab to move, Enter to press.
If you can reach everything, the UI is *keyboard accessible*.
